    Chapter 1 · the 30-second version

    One question, two fixes, one honest answer about cost.

    The question

    Does error mitigation make a noisy quantum circuit's answer more accurate, and what does it cost in extra circuits and shots?

    What we did

    Simulated random circuits on 2, 4 and 6 qubits at four noise levels, and estimated each answer five ways from the same shots — then applied the same idea to a quantum classifier.

    What we found

    At moderate noise, combining both fixes cut the error by a median %. At low noise, the cheap readout fix alone was best.

    One real circuit, four estimates

    n = 4 · L = 4 · moderate noise

    What to notice

    • Noise pulls the answer toward zero. The unmitigated estimate is far below the exact value.
    • REM fixes the measurement part, ZNE fixes most of the gate part, and both together get closest.
    • None of them is free: every fix costs extra circuits or extra shots.
    Say it like this

    "Noise shrinks the expectation values we measure. We compared readout error mitigation, zero-noise extrapolation, and the two combined, against doing nothing, and measured both how much error each removes and what it costs."

    Chapter 2 · what we actually measure

    A quantum answer is an average over many shots.

    Each shot runs the circuit once and returns a bitstring. We score each bitstring +1 if it has an even number of 1s and −1 if odd. The average of those scores is the parity, our answer. Fire some shots and watch the average settle.

    Shot machine

    example 2-qubit circuit, exact parity 0.50
    even number of 1s scores +1odd scores −1the readout flipped a bit

    What to notice

    • Few shots give a jumpy average; more shots tighten it. That leftover wobble is shot noise, about ±0.03 at 1,024 shots.
    • Turn up the readout flips: the average settles on a smaller number. More shots cannot fix that, because it is bias.
    • Parity reacts to every flip on any qubit, which is why we chose it: it makes noise, and its removal, easy to see.
    Say it like this

    "Our observable is the parity, the average of plus or minus one over all shots. Any single bit flip changes its sign, so it is the most noise-sensitive thing we could measure, which makes mitigation effects clearly visible."

    Chapter 3 · the two kinds of noise

    Gates scramble the state; the readout misreports it.

    Every gate has a small chance of depolarizing the qubits it touches, and every measurement has a small chance of reporting the wrong bit. Both shrink the parity. Change the circuit size and the error rates to see which one eats more of the signal.

    What to notice

    • Readout loss depends only on n: the parity is multiplied by exactly (1 − 2p_ro)ⁿ.
    • Gate loss grows with the number of gates, so it grows with both n and L. Deep circuits are dominated by gate noise.
    • In our full simulation, readout's share of the bias fell from % at low noise to % at moderate noise, as gate noise grows with depth faster than readout error does.
    Say it like this

    "There are two error sources. Gate noise grows with circuit size and depth; readout error depends only on how many qubits we measure. For shallow circuits they were about equal; for deeper ones gate noise dominated."

    Chapter 4 · readout error mitigation (REM)

    Measure how the readout blurs known states, then undo the blur.

    We prepare every basis state (00, 01, 10, 11 …), measure each many times, and record what comes out. That gives the calibration matrix A. Real results are blurred by A, so we multiply them by A⁻¹ to un-blur them.

    A real calibration matrix from our experiment

    Column = state we prepared, row = what we measured. The diagonal is "read correctly"; everything off it is readout error. Each column sums to 1.

    The catch: calibration cost

    full REM needs 2ⁿ calibration circuits

    The scalable version: tensored REM

    2 calibration circuits, any n

    If each qubit's readout error is independent of the others, the n-qubit assignment matrix is the Kronecker product of n 2×2 single-qubit matrices. Measuring those single-qubit matrices takes only 2 calibration circuits total, no matter how large n is — against 2ⁿ for full REM. We ran both on every condition and compared the corrected expectation values directly.

    Mean absolute difference between tensored and full REM: , maximum — both well inside the per-condition shot-noise floor, so the uncorrelated-error assumption cost us nothing here while cutting calibration circuits from to at n = 6.

    What to notice

    • With exact numbers REM recovers the truth perfectly. With finite shots it is close but noisy, and can produce small negative probabilities.
    • We take the expectation value from the raw, possibly negative vector, and only clip it to a real distribution for fidelity metrics.
    • Neither REM variant touches gate noise. Full REM's cost doubles with every qubit ( circuits at n = 6); tensored REM stays at a constant , trading exactness for scalability.
    Say it like this

    "REM calibrates the readout by preparing every basis state, builds the assignment matrix, and inverts it. Full calibration grows as two to the n, so we also implemented a tensored version that assumes independent per-qubit readout errors and needs only two calibration circuits; it matched full REM within shot noise on every condition we tried."

    Chapter 5 · zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE)

    Make the noise worse on purpose, then extrapolate back to zero.

    We cannot turn noise off, but we can turn it up. Folding replaces the circuit U with U·U†·U: the same ideal answer, three times the gates, three times the noise. Measure at 1×, 3× and 5× noise, fit a curve, and read it off at zero.

    What to notice

    • Doing nothing gives a narrow but wrong answer. ZNE gives a roughly right but wide one: the extrapolation weights (15/8, −5/4, 3/8) multiply the shot noise by about ×.
    • Measurements are not folded, so readout error is the same at every λ and ZNE cannot remove it.
    • Folded circuits must never be optimized: the compiler would cancel each U†·U pair and the noise would not grow.
    Say it like this

    "ZNE runs the circuit at one, three and five times the noise by folding it, then extrapolates back to zero with Richardson extrapolation. It removes most of the gate-noise bias, but it amplifies shot noise by about two and a half times and leaves readout error untouched."

    Chapter 8 · does mitigation help a downstream task?

    Noise shrinks the margin long before it flips the label.

    Track A asks whether mitigation fixes a number. Track B asks whether that matters for a decision: a variational quantum classifier (VQC) on the Iris binary task, scored with angle-encoded features and a layered ry–rz–CX ansatz, trained once on exact simulation and then evaluated at each noise level with each of the four mitigation methods — on the same train/test split as a classical Logistic Regression and SVM baseline.

    Accuracy barely moves; the margin tells the real story

    With only 20 test samples, accuracy moves in 5% steps and cannot resolve differences that the continuous expectation-value and margin metrics show clearly. A classifier can keep answering correctly even while the number underneath it is drifting a lot — right up until the margin collapses far enough to flip a prediction.

    What to notice

    • Track B reuses the same four mitigation methods as Track A (no tensored REM there), applied to the same kind of noisy expectation values that feed the classifier's decision function.
    • Accuracy is a coarse, thresholded metric; margin retention and mean |E| error are continuous and move far more sensitively with noise and mitigation.
    • The VQC is not trained to be noise-robust; it is trained once on exact simulation, so everything we measure is the effect of noise and mitigation at evaluation time.
    Say it like this

    "We also mitigated a downstream task, not just a number: a variational classifier against Logistic Regression and SVM on identical inputs. Noise collapses the decision margin well before it flips enough labels to move accuracy, so margin retention and expectation error are where mitigation's benefit actually shows up."

    Chapter 9 · two improvements on the baseline recipe

    Spend the same budget smarter, not just more of it.

    Two add-ons were tested against the plain Track A recipe: the tensored REM already covered in chapter 4, and a variance-optimal shot allocation for ZNE.

    Uniform vs. optimal shot allocation

    same total shot budget

    Richardson extrapolation's coefficients γ₁, γ₃, γ₅ are not equal in size, so spending shots evenly across λ = 1, 3, 5 is not the variance-minimal choice at fixed total shots. Allocating shots in proportion to |γᵢ|·σᵢ instead minimizes the extrapolated estimator's variance for the same total cost.

    Measured standard-deviation ratio (optimal ÷ uniform): , close to the theoretical prediction — about an 11% reduction in standard deviation at equal budget, confirmed empirically over repetitions per condition.

    What to notice

    • Both improvements are “free” in the sense that they change how a fixed budget is spent, not how much is spent.
    • Tensored REM trades a small, measured amount of exactness (an uncorrelated-readout assumption) for circuit count that no longer scales with n.
    • Optimal shot allocation needs an estimate of each λ's shot-noise std, which the uniform pilot run already gives you for free before you reallocate.
    Say it like this

    "On top of the baseline comparison, we tried two improvements: tensored REM, which drops calibration cost from two-to-the-n to a constant two circuits by assuming independent readout errors, and variance-optimal shot allocation for ZNE, which reduces the Richardson estimator's standard deviation by about 11% at the same total shot budget."

    Chapter 10 · what the study can't tell you

    Know the limits before someone asks.

    Mitigation is not correction

    It improves averages only. Every individual shot is still noisy, and no extra qubits protect the information.

    ZNE trades bias for variance

    Its shot noise is about ×–× larger (theory ×), its extrapolation curve can mismatch the real decay, and it never removes readout error, since measurements are not folded.

    Folded circuits are up to × deeper

    On real hardware the deepest folded circuit may run past the qubits' coherence time, and the noise may not scale as cleanly with folding as it does in simulation.

    Full REM costs 2ⁿ circuits

    Fine for 6 qubits, impossible for 50. We implemented and measured the fix: tensored REM uses a constant calibration circuits by assuming independent per-qubit readout errors, and matched full REM within shot noise (mean |diff| ) on every condition we ran — but that assumption is untested here against devices with correlated readout crosstalk.

    REM assumes stable, independent readout

    Real readout drifts over time and can depend on neighbouring qubits. Tensored REM additionally assumes qubit-to-qubit independence, which held in simulation but is a stronger assumption on real hardware. Our calibration also includes a little state-preparation error, so it slightly overstates the readout error.

    Simulation only

    The noise model is incoherent and memoryless. Real devices add coherent errors, crosstalk, T1/T2 relaxation, drift and leakage.

    Small sample

    Three qubit counts, two depths and five seeds per Track A condition (20 test samples for Track B). Pooled tests are strong; several per-condition tests are not significant.

    Selection bias

    We kept only circuits whose exact parity is at least 0.3 in size (observed range –), so circuits with near-zero answers are not covered.

    Simulator time is not hardware time

    Wall time measures the classical simulation. Circuits and shots are the meaningful cost.
